The Trading Town has no name that all its inhabitants share. Settlers, sailors, and merchants have called it many things over the centuries, and the native people who were here before any of them arrived have their own name for it still. What everyone agrees on is its importance: it is the economic heart of the known world, a place where goods, people, and information arrive from every direction and leave again changed.

## Description
---
The town occupies a natural valley where a plains biome slopes northeast toward a sheltered bay. The terrain is uneven and layered, with the harbor at its lowest point and the land rising in tiers toward the plains above. A sheer cliff runs along the northern edge of the valley, reaching nearly eighty blocks above sea level at its highest point. It is this geography that made the location so desirable: the bay provides calm anchorage, the cliffs provide natural defense and building material, and the surrounding plains provide farmland enough to feed a settlement of meaningful size.

The harbor dominates the lower town. Ships dock along its southern edge, where a crane arm extending from [[Brownigan & Sons|the factory]] at the southwestern corner handles the movement of heavy cargo between vessel and yard. A lighthouse stands on a stony peninsula at the bay's mouth, visible from a considerable distance out to sea. The docks tavern sits at water level near the harbor mouth, connected through the cliff face to a [[Happy Ghast Landing|landing and docking station]] for travelers arriving by air.

Above the harbor, the town climbs the hillside along a main street that begins at the water and curves southwest before turning south toward the base of the upper plains. Buildings of various ages and purposes line the street and its branching roads, with housing mixed throughout rather than separated from commerce. The upper plains beyond the hill's crest are given over to farmland, worked by families who have lived on that ground for generations.

The [[The Trade Yard|trade yard]] sits on an elevated plateau above the eastern harbor, carved partly into the cliffside. It is the formal mechanism through which all commerce in the town is processed, documented, and taxed.

## History
---
A native village occupied this site long before the first colonial settlers arrived, its people having lived along the bay and in the shadow of the surrounding mountains for as long as memory holds. Among them a hereditary class of [[The Cartographers|cartographers]] maintained an ongoing record of how the land changed over time, a practice tied to beliefs the settlers never fully understood and largely did not ask about.

Contact between the natives and the arriving colonists was peaceful. Trade and intermarriage followed, and for a time the two communities existed alongside each other with reasonable goodwill. That goodwill proved a foundation for something more damaging than hostility might have been. The settlers needed land, then more land, and the gradual processes of displacement, intermarriage, and cultural abandonment did the work that open conflict never had to. Many native families absorbed into the colonial town lost their traditions across a generation or two without any single moment anyone could point to as the cause. The loss is ongoing in its effects even now.

[[Penny Brixen|Brix]], a practical and unsentimental woman who arrived with the early settlers, established the customs and trade apparatus that still underpins the town's economy. She had no interest in what came after and left once the work was done. [[Josiah Juxton]] moved quickly into the institutional space she vacated, formalizing civic authority around the trade infrastructure she had built and establishing a family hold on town governance that persisted across generations. He served as the town's first merrif, combining the roles of civic head and law enforcement in the way that early settlements almost always required of a single person.

[[Brownigan & Sons]] grew alongside the town from a modest founding workshop into its dominant industrial force across three generations of the Brownigan family. The factory's crane and its control over the harbor's cargo throughput gave it structural influence over the town's economy that no formal authority ever fully countered.

## Culture
---
The town is a colonial settlement that has largely forgotten it is one. The native village still exists on the western mountain, its cartographers still working, but most residents of the main town would describe its history as simply the history of its founding families. The edges of that story, what was here before, what was lost in the building of the place, are not discussed in any formal way. They surface sometimes in the older native families, in the lighthouse keeper's silence on certain subjects, in traditions that persist without anyone being entirely sure where they came from.

The harbor gives the town its character more than anything else. It is a place people pass through as much as a place they live, and that transience shapes everything from the tavern culture to the architecture to the way strangers are regarded on the street. Sailors, merchants, and travelers from distant ports are common enough that no one looks twice, which has historically made it a useful place for people who prefer not to be looked at twice.
