The [[Swampfolk]] of [[Reedmarsh]] are the people native to the village at the southern foot of the [[Thornspire Heartwood]], and among the most established human communities within [[The Great Swamp|the Great Swamp]]. They are practical and more open to outside contact than most of their neighbors, though that openness has been tested in recent memory.

## Description
---
Reedmarsh swampfolk build predominantly in spruce, sourced from the Heartwood above the village, giving their structures a darker and heavier character than most settlements of comparable size. Their traditional dress runs to deep greens and purples, colors so long associated with swamp life that few among them would think to explain them. Outsiders who know the swamp well sometimes note the parallel to the green of the [[Verdant Veins]] and the deep purple of [[Mireblood]], though whether this connection is intentional, forgotten, or simply coincidental is not something the Swampfolk themselves tend to discuss.

## Culture
---
Herbalism and fishing are the twin foundations of daily life in Reedmarsh, supplemented by trade in goods unique to the swamp. The community is self-sufficient by instinct and necessity. Reverence for the swamp is not a formal belief system so much as a shared disposition, an understanding passed down through families that the land gives what it gives and asks for measured behavior in return.

Compared to the people of the Thornspire Heartwood, the Swampfolk of Reedmarsh are relatively open to outsiders, a disposition shaped in part by their longer history of trade and outside contact. Their relationship with the Heartwood remains their closest and most longstanding bond, formalized in the [[Pact of Root & Reed]] but rooted in something older than the document itself.

## Relations
---
Reedmarsh trades with several communities, not all of them well documented. Their position within the swamp makes them a natural waypoint for certain kinds of goods moving through the region. The [[Cauldron Tap]] has complicated this. The slow degradation of the swamp it causes is a grievance with no clean outlet, and the community is not unified in how to respond. Older inhabitants carry the memory of what the swamp was before the Tap and tend toward a quiet, watchful resentment. Younger swampfolk are less patient, and some have taken to disrupting the operation in small ways, spoiling shipments, obstructing access routes, making the work inconvenient where they cannot make it impossible. The restraint is not born of indifference but of the plain reality that Reedmarsh does not have the force to do more than that, at least not yet.
