==The native people have no settled name in the articles yet. A name is being developed.== They are the original inhabitants of the land surrounding [[Main Trading Town|the trading town]], predating the colonial settlement by thousands of years. They live on the eastern face of the western mountain above the harbor, oriented toward the open land and the rising sun rather than the sea below. Their relationship to the earth is not spiritual in the way the settlers use that word, it is something closer to the way a careful person reads a face, attentive, practiced, and convinced that what they observe is genuinely communicating something.

## Beliefs
---
The land is understood as a living system whose changes are meaningful. The terrain does not simply shift through geological process. It speaks, slowly, in the language of erosion and growth and collapse, and those changes constitute a form of communication from the earth itself that accumulates meaning across generations of observation. This belief is not metaphor to them. It is the operating framework through which the world makes sense.

Their spatial thinking is anchored to absolute cardinal directions tied to the landscape rather than relative ones. They do not say turn left. They say go toward the mountain, or go toward the sunrise. East is their primary orientation, the direction of the rising sun and the open land ahead of them, with the mountain at their backs to the west grounding and anchoring them. Their maps, hung on walls, are oriented with east at the top. Maps laid flat follow the real world.

The caves that riddle the plains and flatlands surrounding the town are understood as thresholds, places where the boundary between the surface world and the interior of the living earth is thin. They do not go below ground. This prohibition is ancient and absolute, rooted in an encounter their ancestors had in the cave systems below the town so long ago that no written account of it exists. What survives is mythology, and what the mythology describes is a sleeping presence beneath the earth that their ancestors approached before they were ready, and from which they fled. The settler practice of covering cave entrances, done for practical reasons without any understanding of their significance, is experienced as an unknowing act of desecration, the mouths of something sacred being sealed by people who do not know what they are touching.

## Society
---
The village organizes itself around the [[The Cartographers|cartographers]], a hereditary class whose practice of mapping the land over time is the community's central cultural function. Everything else supports that work across generations. The cartographers are not governors or priests, they are the people who pay attention on behalf of everyone else, and the community understands its role as making that attention possible.

Intermarriage with the settler population is regarded with deep unease, not through hostility toward individuals but through a clear eyed understanding of what tends to follow. The generations that result from such unions typically lose the language, the practice, and the framework through which the world makes sense to the native community. That loss is experienced as a kind of death that happens too slowly to fight directly. The community does not cast out those who choose it, but they grieve the choice.

## Relationship to the Settlement
---
Contact with the arriving settlers was mostly peaceful at the outset, though not without friction. What followed was not conquest in any clean sense but something more diffuse and in some ways harder to resist. Land was absorbed gradually. Families intermarried. Traditions that required community to sustain them withered as the community shrank. The loss is ongoing. The village on the mountain still stands, its cartographers still working, but the land below that was once theirs now belongs to people who arrived a few centuries ago and have largely stopped thinking about that fact.

The worn pathways circumnavigating the mountain, cut into the rock over thousands of years of continuous use, predate the colonial town entirely. The settlers use some of them without knowing what they are.
